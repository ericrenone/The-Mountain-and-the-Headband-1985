# 🐒 The Mountain and the Headband

### Steve Jobs, Akira Toriyama, and the Year Both Men Turned Thirty

*Two men born forty days apart in 1955 reached the same fork in 1985. One was thrown out of the company he built and spent twelve years rebuilding it from the outside. The other stayed in his chair, drew a weekly comic for another decade, and turned a monkey from a Ming-dynasty novel into the most copied hero in the world. This is the full comparison: births, teachers, breakthroughs, the hinge year, the long middle, the returns, the deaths, and what each left behind.*

## 2. The Framework: The Pilgrim's Arc

Most comparisons between two famous creators sort them into categories (taste versus laziness, showman versus recluse). This one follows a path instead, borrowed from the book Toriyama chose as the spine of *Dragon Ball*. *Journey to the West* is a story with a very specific shape. A stone-born rebel gains power, overreaches, is imprisoned for centuries under a mountain, is released on condition of discipline, and then walks a long road to retrieve something of value for other people. The shape fits both men uncannily well. The framework below uses it as a seven-station ruler.

| Station | What the myth does | What happens in a creator's life | Jobs | Toriyama |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **I. Stone** | Wukong is born from a rock, without parents or training | Origins that carry no obvious ticket to the future | Adopted in San Francisco; Los Altos garage culture | Rural Aichi, no artists in the family, a design-course high school |
| **II. The Borrowed Ruler** | He studies under a master, gains immortality and techniques from another school | A discipline from next door supplies a standard | Calligraphy, Zen, Bauhaus | Flyers, lettering, model kits, kung-fu films |
| **III. Rebellion** | He storms Heaven, takes the peaches, defeats the army | A breakthrough product that bends an industry | Apple II, then Macintosh | *Dr. Slump*, then *Dragon Ball* |
| **IV. The Hinge** | He meets a power he cannot beat and is trapped | A forced fork at about thirty | Resignation, September 1985 | Thirty years old, a year into a weekly grind, a game offer he did not want |
| **V. Under the Mountain** | Five centuries pinned beneath the Five Elements Mountain | A long middle where the old tools stop working | NeXT, Pixar | Weekly deadlines, the *Dragon Ball Z* machine, a hated treadmill |
| **VI. The Scripture Run** | He is freed, accepts a headband, and walks to India | A disciplined return that serves others | Apple again, 1997 to 2011 | Chrono Trigger, Sand Land, delegating *Dragon Ball Super* |
| **VII. Ascension** | Buddhahood is conferred after the last trial | Death and what outlives it | October 5, 2011 | March 1, 2024 |

Two details of the myth help organize what follows. The mountain is punishment, but it is also incubation: Wukong comes out of it changed. And the headband that Xuanzang's disciple wears is a constraint that makes the journey possible. Both of these show up in the 1985 hinge, and they come back in Section 12.

## 3. Station I: Stone (1955 to the early 1970s)

### The births

Steven Paul Jobs was born in San Francisco on February 24, 1955, to Joanne Schieble and Abdulfattah Jandali, and was placed in a closed adoption with machinist Paul Jobs and bookkeeper Clara Jobs. The family moved from Mountain View to Crist Drive in Los Altos in 1967, in what was becoming Silicon Valley.

Akira Toriyama was born forty days later, on April 5, 1955, in Kiyosu, a small city in Aichi Prefecture outside Nagoya, where he would live and work for the rest of his life. He had a younger sister. As he put it, "My father, mother, and little sister don't draw at all."

### Media from across the ocean

Both grew up inside the postwar consumer-media boom, and both were formed by culture from the opposite side of the Pacific:

- **Jobs** soaked in Hewlett-Packard parts catalogs. At 13 he cold-called Bill Hewlett for components and landed a summer job at HP. From 1970 he was a regular at the Haiku Zendo in Los Altos, where Sōtō priest Kōbun Chino Otogawa taught.
- **Toriyama** was set on the path to drawing by Disney's *One Hundred and One Dalmatians* (1961). He read *Astro Boy*, *Gigantor*, and *Osomatsu-kun*, drifted to *Ultraman* and *Gamera* in middle school, and built plastic models, especially military kits.

### First observation

The Pacific ran in both directions. The American boy's earliest teachers on taste were Japanese and European (a Zen priest, later Braun and the Bauhaus). The Japanese boy's earliest teachers were American and Chinese (Disney, then Hollywood science fiction and Hong Kong action films). Each man's "native" aesthetic was partly the other's culture, refined and sent back.

## 4. Station II: The Borrowed Ruler (1970 to 1977)

### Jobs: a monk, a pen, and the width of a space

Jobs enrolled at Reed College in Portland in fall 1972, was officially a student for one semester, then stayed about eighteen months as an auditor, sleeping in Westport Hall, repairing equipment for a psychology professor, and taking whatever he wanted. One thing he wanted was calligraphy.

The calligraphy program was the product of a forty-year project by Lloyd J. Reynolds, who taught at Reed from 1929 to 1969 and adopted the Italic hand because it resembles printed type. His doctrine could pass for interface-design theory: "A letter is a letter, not a picture of anything." But Reynolds had retired three years before Jobs arrived. The teacher in the room was **Robert Palladino**, a former Trappist monk who had left his order in 1968 and taught Reynolds's method to about eighty students a year. Palladino never used a computer. Later he said of Jobs, "He introduced me to the mouse."

Jobs told the story at Stanford on June 12, 2005. What he said he learned was specific: serif versus sans-serif, "varying the amount of space between different letter combinations," and what makes typography great. Ten years later, he said, it "all came back" in the Macintosh, which would otherwise never have had multiple typefaces or proportionally spaced fonts. In other words, calligraphy gave him no content to ship. It gave him a ruler that no one in the personal-computer business owned.

He accumulated other rulers on the same pattern:

- **Zen.** Kōbun Chino Otogawa became his teacher. The story goes that when Jobs announced "I feel I'm enlightened," Kōbun replied that enlightenment must be shown with proof, and a week later Jobs returned with the first Apple computer. Whether that is reportage or parable, the standard is clear: no claim counts until an object exists.
- **Markkula.** In 1977 Mike Markkula wrote "The Apple Marketing Philosophy," built on three words: Empathy, Focus, Impute. Jobs cited it for decades.
- **Aspen.** After first attending the International Design Conference in June 1981, Jobs absorbed Bauhaus functionalism. In 1983 he told the same conference that computers risked becoming "one more piece-of-junk-object" and that the aim was packaging "just like Braun does with its electronics," explicitly rejecting Sony's "high-tech" look.

### Toriyama: a rooster that was the wrong sex

Toriyama's ruler was the flyer. He attended a high school with a design course because he wanted to be a graphic designer, and after school spent about three years at a Nagoya design firm producing, in his words, "almost all flyers." It was not a happy job. He got home at two or three in the morning, could not get up, and was late so often that three late arrivals counted as a missed day. He was reprimanded for his clothes. In his third year his pay was still low. A client once rejected a poster chicken with the line, "This is a rooster, so it's no good. We only sell hens."

He quit at 23. No primary source says he was fired. Years later he said, "I hated it at the time, but having that experience behind me, I think it might have come in handy." In 1980 he was more defensive: "I was forced to do things I'm not good with, like lettering."

The flyer taught him what a poster teaches anyone. A flyer has no reader, only a passer-by, so every mark must work at a distance, at a glance, with no goodwill. He hated uneven lines for the rest of his life: "I hate it when the linework is uneven, or the art is poor." And with no money for screentone early on, he learned to work in solid black and white, the idiom of the poster rather than the illustrated page.

His other rulers were film and plastic. *101 Dalmatians* gave him the closed contour. Model kits gave him a mechanical conscience ("Nothing is wasted in the designs"). Hong Kong cinema gave him pacing. He saw *Drunken Master* dozens of times and said "If I hadn't seen this movie, I would never have come up with Dragon Ball."

### The pattern

| | Jobs | Toriyama |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Teacher who did not shape the product | A monk teaching italic hand | A client who only sold hens |
| The standard supplied | Letter-pair spacing, proportion, quality | The unambiguous mark at a distance |
| What insiders could not see | Kerning, in a field of engineers | Uneven linework, in a field of storytellers |
| Self-assessment | "No hope of any practical application" | "Might have come in handy" |

In both cases the adjacent discipline supplied the standard, not the content.

## 5. Station III: Rebellion (1976 to 1984)

### Apple: from garage to Macintosh

- **April 1, 1976.** Apple founded in the Crist Drive house with Steve Wozniak and Ronald Wayne. The Byte Shop orders 50 Apple I units at $500 each.
- **1977.** The Apple II appears; its first brochure carries the line "Simplicity is the ultimate sophistication." Markkula writes his philosophy.
- **1979.** Jobs visits Xerox PARC and sees the graphical interface. CEO Mike Scott removes him from the Lisa and he joins the Macintosh team.
- **December 12, 1980.** Apple goes public.
- **August 1983.** Jobs walks through the software group, looks at the font menu Susan Kare and Andy Hertzfeld named after commuter stops, and demands "WORLD CLASS cities." The fonts become Chicago, New York, Geneva, London, San Francisco, Toronto, and Venice.
- **December 1983.** He convinces Adobe's John Warnock to license PostScript.
- **January 22 and 24, 1984.** The Ridley Scott "1984" spot airs during the Super Bowl; the Macintosh is introduced two days later, with a 1920 Hashiguchi Goyō woodblock print on its screen.

Everything on the original Mac was an exercise in the narrowest pipe: 128 KB of memory and a one-bit screen. Chicago has no jagged diagonals because a one-bit display cannot draw them. Jobs did not draw a pixel; his contribution was the "no" applied to what was not yet world-class.

### Toriyama: from rejection to *Dr. Slump* to *Dragon Ball*

- **1977.** A *Star Wars* parody is submitted to *Weekly Shōnen Jump*'s newcomer contest. It is ineligible because parodies are not allowed. Editor Kazuhiko Torishima finds it "beautiful and funny" and telegrams the author to keep drawing and send work directly to him. Torishima later explained why: "You were good at hand-lettering."
- **1977–78.** About 500 pages are rejected. Toriyama puts the number before his 1978 debut; Torishima puts it in the eighteen months before *Dr. Slump*. Either way, the number is remembered as a number. Toriyama's motive was stubbornness: "I was confident in my art, so the fact that I lost out was really frustrating."
- **1978.** *Wonder Island* debuts and finishes last in the reader survey. The sequel flops.
- **1980.** *Dr. Slump* begins. Torishima insists the lead should be the robot girl Arale, not the inventor Senbei. Toriyama's first response: "I don't wanna." Later he agreed it "turned out better." The villain Dr. Mashirito exists because the first draft had "no unique traits. Make him even worse." The name is Torishima's syllables reversed.
- **1981.** *Dr. Slump* wins the Shogakukan Manga Award; the anime begins on Fuji TV.
- **May 2, 1982.** Toriyama marries manga artist Yoshimi Katō, who works as Nachi Mikami.
- **1983.** Prototype one-shots appear: *Dragon Boy* (August and October *Fresh Jump*) and *The Adventure of Tongpoo* (*Weekly Shōnen Jump* issue 52, November 29, 1983). When he wanted to end *Dr. Slump*, Torishima set a condition: start a new serial three months later and you may end the old one.
- **1984.** *Dr. Slump* ends. After a research trip to China in spring, *Dragon Ball* begins on November 20, 1984 (cover date December 3). It is loosely built on *Journey to the West*, with a tailed boy as the Monkey King.

When early *Dragon Ball* underperformed, Torishima told Toriyama the hero was "rather plain." It annoyed the artist. It also produced the martial-arts tournament format that carried the series.

### The numbered no

Jobs and Toriyama each worked inside a system that said no with a quantity attached.

| | Apple | *Weekly Shōnen Jump* |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| The instrument | Markkula's three words; the market | The weekly reader survey |
| The interpreter | Jobs, by temperament; later the board | Torishima |
| The veto | "Is this a distraction?" "Does the box look like an afterthought?" | A last-place ranking |
| The doctrine | Focus and Impute | A "half step into the future" hypothesis |
| Who holds the veto | A strong founder | Child readers mailing in cards; the editor-in-chief's view is "basically irrelevant" when readers respond strongly |

Torishima's refinement is the part that matters: a survey reflects one moment, so the editor must form a hypothesis ahead of the number. Veto by data, direction by judgment. Markkula's Empathy is the corporate cousin.

By the end of 1984 both men had shipped the product that defined them, inside the same calendar year, under different kinds of pressure. The next year, the pressure on one of them became unbearable.

## 6. Station IV: The Hinge Year, 1985

Both men turned thirty in 1985. Jobs did on February 24; Toriyama on April 5. The year divided each of their lives into a before and an after, though only one of the divisions was visible from outside.

### Month by month

| When | Steve Jobs and Apple | Akira Toriyama and Japan |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **January** | January 23: the LaserWriter ($6,995) is announced, with PostScript and AppleTalk; Aldus announces PageMaker the same day, and "desktop publishing" gets its name. The Macintosh is a year old. | *Dragon Ball* is two months into weekly serialization, a fourteen-page-a-week treadmill with one assistant. |
| **February** | A *Playboy* interview with the line "We built it for ourselves." | The series continues to find its footing. |
| **April** | | April 5: Toriyama turns 30. |
| **Spring** | Jobs, unhappy with Sculley and Apple's direction, is widely reported to have tried to remove him while Sculley was traveling; the board sided with Sculley. | The series is finding its shape; the tournament format Torishima pushed him toward becomes its engine. |
| **September 13** | | Nintendo's *Super Mario Bros.* ships in Japan, four days before Jobs resigns. Miyamoto's Mario is a 16×16 sprite. |
| **September 17** | Jobs submits his resignation after Sculley persuades the board to remove him from operations. He is 30. | |
| **Late September** | Jobs founds NeXT with a small group from Apple, and invites Kōbun to be its "spiritual advisor." | |
| **November** | Over the following weeks Jobs sells almost all of his Apple stock. Microsoft releases Windows 1.0 on November 20. | Yuji Horii and Koichi Nakamura begin development of *Dragon Quest* on the Famicom. Toriyama is the character designer. |

### What the two Septembers have in common

1985 gave each man a thing he did not want.

- **Jobs wanted to keep running Apple.** He instead got a resignation letter and a company that was no longer his. He called it being fired ("How can you get fired from a company you started?"), and the word stuck, but formally it was a resignation. He was thirty.
- **Toriyama wanted to draw one comic.** He instead got the beginning of a second job. Torishima, who edited both Toriyama and Horii, had engineered a video game that *Jump* could cover exclusively and had pitched Toriyama as its artist. Horii was told "Toriyama wants to work on this." Toriyama later said, "I never said that. Torishima ordered me to do it." His own printed account is that he did not quite understand the concept but accepted because of Torishima's insistence and some side money. "I wasn't all that knowledgeable about fantasy worlds in the first place," he wrote.

One was removed from a role; the other was added to one. In both cases a powerful older figure (Sculley and the board for Jobs, Torishima for Toriyama) rearranged the pieces. Both complied, for different reasons, and in both cases the compliance became the foundation of the next decade.

### The two older figures

| | John Sculley | Kazuhiko Torishima |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Recruited by or recruited | Recruited by Jobs in 1983 | Discovered Toriyama in 1977 |
| Relationship to the creator | Became the person who displaced him | Became the person who shaped him |
| Instrument | The board | The reader survey |
| What the creator learned | That a founder can be removed | That a hit can be made to order |
| The outcome | Jobs left and built NeXT | Toriyama stayed and built *Dragon Ball*, then *Dragon Quest* |

The contrast is instructive. Both Sculley and Torishima exercised enormous authority over a younger genius. The difference is that Torishima's authority was legitimized by a number the artist could not argue with, while Sculley's was legitimized by a vote the founder could not win. Toriyama even drew his editor into the comic as the villain, and Torishima, by his own account, embraced it. A founder who cannot do that to his CEO may not survive the relationship.

### The world in 1985

The year was not only about them. In Japan, the Family Computer (¥14,800 at its 1983 launch) was the best-selling console and the place where the next generation of games, and their characters, would be drawn on tiny grids. In the United States, the Mac was a year old and its early sales had slowed, and desktop publishing, which would rescue it, had just been named. Dragon Quest's director, Yuji Horii, wrote in 1986 about wanting people to "get used to the idea of a game you play by reading text." The two men were, without knowing it, both in the business of teaching strangers a new grammar.

## 7. Station V: Under the Mountain (1985 to 1995/97)

The mountain in the novel is also a kind of school. Wukong sits under it for five hundred years. For Jobs and Toriyama, the decade or more that followed 1985 was a period of apparent constraint in which each of them did the work that later looked like preparation.

### Jobs: NeXT, Pixar, and the long apprenticeship

**NeXT.** Founded in 1985 with the aim of building a powerful workstation for universities, NeXT launched the NeXT Computer on October 12, 1988, and began shipping in 1990 at $9,999. It was beautiful and expensive and did not sell in large numbers. What it did build mattered far more than its sales:

- **NeXTSTEP,** the operating system, which would become the core of Mac OS X and later of iOS.
- **The first web.** In 1990 Tim Berners-Lee built the first web browser and server on a NeXT machine at CERN.
- **Games.** id Software developed *Doom* and *Quake* on NeXT workstations. (Toriyama's own world was digital too, as Section 11 shows.)

**The logo.** Jobs hired Paul Rand to design NeXT's logo for a flat $100,000. Rand delivered a black cube with the name stacked in two lines and the "e" in lowercase, presented in a privately printed booklet. Jobs said Rand gave them "a jewel, which in retrospect seems so obvious." A decade later he called Rand "the greatest living graphic designer." Obvious is what a signal looks like once the noise is gone.

**Pixar.** In 1986 Jobs bought a controlling interest in Lucasfilm's computer-animation unit for $10 million total and carried it for nine years before *Toy Story*, the first fully computer-animated feature, arrived in 1995. Pixar's IPO made him a billionaire. In January 2006, Disney bought Pixar for $7.4 billion in stock, making Jobs Disney's largest shareholder.

**Kōbun.** Jobs's teacher presided at his wedding to Laurene Powell in Yosemite on March 18, 1991. Kōbun's standard of proof remained the unspoken test of everything Jobs built.

**What the mountain taught.** The NeXT years are usually told as a failure with a lucky ending. A closer reading says the hardware failed because Jobs was designing for his own taste without the check of an outside judge. At Apple, Markkula's three words and a board kept him honest; at NeXT, he held the veto himself. NeXT's best output was software, and its best lesson was cost: a beautiful object that nobody could afford was a prototype, not a product. The corrective arrived later in a different form, as the iMac's $1,299 price and the iPod's $399.

### Toriyama: the weekly mountain

For Toriyama, the mountain was a deadline: about fourteen pages a week, one assistant, one hour of sleep. He later described the pace as "very hard" and said he "really didn't like it." Three streams of work ran through the decade.

**1. *Dragon Ball* itself.** The series grew from a *Journey to the West* riff into an escalating tournament epic. Along the way:

- **1986.** The *Dragon Ball* anime debuts (February).
- **1989.** *Dragon Ball Z* premieres as an anime.
- **Early 1990s.** The Super Saiyan transformation appears. The hair, black in the story, is left uninked in the black-and-white manga to save a pass, and it becomes the most legible power-up in comics.
- **1992.** Torishima moves to *V Jump* and Yuu Kondou becomes editor. Toriyama creates Cell after being disappointed with the Androids as villains.
- **1995.** *Dragon Ball* ends after 519 chapters in 42 volumes. *Jump*'s circulation peaks at a record 6.53 million. Toriyama said later, "I wanted to quit because I was exhausted."

**2. *Dragon Quest*.** The one project that sat outside the weekly machine. Horii and Nakamura began development in November 1985; the first *Jump* feature ran on February 11, 1986; the game shipped on the Famicom on May 27, 1986. Toriyama designed the hero, the Dragonlord, the princess, and the monsters, and wrote no story or dialogue. The Slime is the cleanest case study in reduction because the brief survives: Horii asked for "no fixed shape," a gelatinous goo that smothers faces. Toriyama first drew a pixel-art version and discarded it for lacking his flavor. What he sent back was a teardrop with two dots and a curved smile. Horii called it "perfect."

The inversion is total. Horror became cuteness; a shapeless thing became the most fixed silhouette in Japanese games; a face-hugger became a face. Toriyama's stated principle for sprite-era design is a rule about scale: a character must be recognizable "even when they're small." The Slime has needed no redesign since 1986.

**3. Everything else.** *Chrono Trigger* (1995, on Square's "Dream Team" with Hironobu Sakaguchi and Horii), the 1996 3D fighting game *Tobal No. 1*, and a long tail of one-shots.

**What the mountain taught.** The weekly grind forced the convenience gradient: economize where the reader will not notice, escalate where they will. He built a fictional world because "if I made the real world the setting, I'd have to draw looking at reference materials." He gave characters flight because planes and cars were "really tiresome." He did not plot ahead. Read as engineering, every one of these removed a recurring cost from a pipeline while preserving legibility. But he also kept the counter-rule: a fight drawn "on the same level as the previous fight" makes readers think the level has gone down.

### Under the mountain: side by side

| | Jobs, 1985–1997 | Toriyama, 1985–1995 |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Constraint** | Money, a niche market, no outside judge | Deadline, a reader survey, a single assistant |
| **Main vehicle** | NeXT (hardware that did not sell) | *Dragon Ball* (a comic that sold enormously) |
| **Side vehicle** | Pixar (a bet on a different medium) | *Dragon Quest* (a bet on a different medium) |
| **Spiritual anchor** | Kōbun, who also officiated his wedding | A wife who suggested names and gave honest reactions |
| **Perceived status** | Outcast, once a prodigy | Superstar, privately exhausted |
| **What was built that outlasted the product** | NeXTSTEP, the web's first browser, Pixar | The Slime, Goku's silhouette, the shōnen template |
| **Emotional cost** | Public humiliation, private rebuilding | "I wanted to quit because I was exhausted" |
| **How it ended** | A buyout by the company that fired him | A self-declared stop in 1995 |

The two situations look opposite from outside. Jobs was out of power but free to experiment; Toriyama was at the peak of fame but trapped by his own success. Both were pinned. And both used the side project (Pixar, *Dragon Quest*) to learn what a different medium demanded.

## 8. Station VI: The Scripture Run (1995 to 2013)

The headband in the myth is a constraint accepted in exchange for freedom. Jobs's return to Apple and Toriyama's return to drawing both came with conditions.

### Jobs: the return to Apple

- **December 1996.** Apple agrees to acquire NeXT for roughly $400 million; the deal closes in February 1997. Jobs comes back as an adviser.
- **August 1997.** Microsoft invests $150 million in Apple at Macworld Boston, a lifeline and a statement.
- **September 16, 1997.** Gil Amelio is gone; Jobs is named interim CEO. The "Think Different" campaign launches the same year.
- **The cut.** He is widely reported to have cleared Apple's product line down to a handful of machines. It is Markkula's Focus applied at industrial scale.
- **1998.** The iMac, at $1,299, in translucent colors, with no floppy drive and no fan. In his own words: "I was adamant that we get rid of the fan." A fan is noise in both senses.
- **January 2000.** He drops "interim" at Macworld. A *Fortune* profile is titled "Apple's One-Dollar-a-Year Man."
- **May 19, 2001.** The first Apple Stores open in Tysons Corner, Virginia and Glendale, California.
- **October 23, 2001.** The iPod: "1,000 songs in your pocket," $399.
- **April 28, 2003.** The iTunes Music Store opens at 99 cents a song.
- **October 2003.** Jobs is diagnosed with a pancreatic neuroendocrine tumor. He has surgery in July 2004, after about nine months of delay.
- **June 12, 2005.** The Stanford commencement address.
- **January 9, 2007.** The iPhone, "literally five years ahead of any other mobile phone."
- **July 2008.** The App Store opens with more than 500 apps.
- **January 27, 2010.** The iPad.
- **August 24, 2011.** "I hereby resign as CEO of Apple." He recommends Tim Cook.

He also kept running Pixar, and when Disney bought it in January 2006 he became its largest individual shareholder.

The operating system under all of it was NeXTSTEP's descendant. The man who was pushed out in 1985 returned with the thing the company needed and could not build.

### Toriyama: stepping away, then stepping back

After 1995 Toriyama did not do what a manga star is expected to do. He did not start the next hit weekly serial. He did a decade of quieter work:

- **1996.** *Dragon Ball GT* airs (not based on his manga). He renders his art in 3D for *Tobal No. 1* and, in December, begins coloring digitally on a Macintosh. It is the one documented point where his tools and Jobs's touch.
- **1997–2000.** *Cowa!*, *Kajika*, *Neko Majin*, and *Sand Land*, with its handcrafted tank, which he "stubbornly insisted on drawing" himself.
- **December 6, 2002.** His only U.S. appearance, at the launch of Viz's *Shonen Jump* in New York.
- **March 27, 2005.** The QVOLT electric microcar he designed goes on sale (nine built).
- **2006.** The Xbox 360 role-playing game *Blue Dragon*.
- **2013.** The Takashimaya exhibition (72,000 visitors in 19 days), the Angoulême 40th Anniversary award, *Dragon Ball Z: Battle of Gods* (March 30), and *Jaco the Galactic Patrolman* (July 13), his last fully self-drawn manga.
- **2015.** *Dragon Ball Super* begins in *V Jump*, drawn by Toyotarou under Toriyama's story direction. *Resurrection 'F'* opens on April 18.

### Two kinds of return

| | Jobs | Toriyama |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **What returned** | His authority over the company | His involvement, but not his hand |
| **Operating mode** | Total control | Selective delegation |
| **Approach to scale** | Built institutions (stores, a platform, a music business) | Built almost nothing beyond Bird Studio and a creative circle |
| **Relationship to the old thing** | Rescued it, then reinvented it | Handed it to a younger artist |
| **Constraint accepted** | Product cuts; a short list of things to do well | A lighter role, with Toyotarou drawing and Toriyama writing |
| **What he gave up** | Other people's comfort | The weekly page |

This is the sharpest divergence. Jobs's second act was an exercise in re-centralizing: fewer products, tighter control, one voice. Toriyama's second act was an exercise in decentralizing: the work no longer needed his hand every week, and he was happy to let it go.

### The convenience gradient, again

Both men ran the same slope in the long middle, in opposite moral registers. Jobs: "Simple can be harder than complex: You have to work hard to get your thinking clean." Toriyama: "I choose my criteria based on what can be easy." The operation is the same, a cost-removal exercise with a quality floor. Jobs removed the fan from the iMac and lavished the box; Toriyama randomized the rocks and escalated the fight. Jony Ive put the price plainly: "To be truly simple, you have to go really deep."

## 9. Station VII: Ascension and Afterlife (2011 to today)

### Jobs

Jobs died on October 5, 2011, at 56, in Palo Alto, six weeks after resigning as CEO. His company's shift into mobile would continue under Tim Cook; the typeface family he had named by the standard of "world class cities" survives in Apple's system faces called **San Francisco** (introduced with Apple Watch in 2015) and **New York** (2019). Posthumously he received the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 2022. In his final decade, the emoji keyboard on his product became the world's most widely used pictographic alphabet (Section 11).

### Toriyama

Toriyama died on March 1, 2024, at 68, of an acute subdural hematoma. His studio announced it on March 8, adding that the funeral had been private, with no flowers, no gifts, no visits, as he had wished. What followed is the best available measure of how far a compressed shape can travel.

- Japan's Chief Cabinet Secretary credited his work with raising Japan's international profile and bringing tourists.
- China's Foreign Ministry expressed condolences. The President of France posted a drawing Toriyama had signed for him.
- Jackie Chan thanked him on Weibo for "creating so many classic works."
- In Buenos Aires, tens of thousands gathered at the obelisk and performed the Genki Dama, Goku's signature gesture, together. No one had to translate it.
- Eiichiro Oda: "His existence is like a big tree." Masashi Kishimoto called the work "salvation" for "a rural boy" like himself.
- His last published contribution, on March 28, 2024, was a redrawn page of a *Dragon Ball Super* chapter.
- In October 2024 *Dragon Ball Daima* premiered with his name on the story and character designs, and he was inducted posthumously into the Harvey Awards Hall of Fame.
- In May 2026 Square Enix announced that *Dragon Quest XII* had been restarted from scratch with Toriyama's art and Koichi Sugiyama's music retained. A series whose artist and composer are both dead will ship its next mainline game with both men's work. The franchise passed 100 million in combined shipments and digital sales in September 2026, the fortieth anniversary year.

### The afterlife comparison

| | Jobs | Toriyama |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **The institution that persists** | Apple | Bird Studio, Shueisha, Square Enix |
| **The artifact that persists** | Products | Characters |
| **The idiom that persists** | The single-purpose, single-surface device | The silhouette and the empty face |
| **Who continues the work** | A successor CEO | A younger artist (Toyotarou) and a game studio |
| **Whose name carries it** | "Jobs" on every keynote's ghost | "Toriyama" on the title page |
| **What the posthumous honors reward** | Civic leadership | Cultural reach |

## 10. Cross-Cutting Comparisons

### 10.1 Design doctrine

| Dimension | Jobs | Toriyama |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Stated principle** | "Design is how it works." ("Most people make the mistake of thinking design is what it looks like.") | "Basically with everything, I choose my criteria based on what can be easy." |
| **Mantra** | "Focus and simplicity." | A character must be recognizable "even when they're small." |
| **Where he moralized** | Simplicity as virtue | Simplicity as expedient |
| **Source of the standard** | Calligraphy, Braun, Rand, Zen | Flyers, model kits, Hong Kong cinema |
| **What he refused to simplify** | Materials, packaging, the experience of opening the box | Machinery, and fight escalation |
| **Test of success** | The object works in the user's hand | The page works when reversed (Jean-Paul Jennequin: "Akira Toriyama's drawings do not lose anything when reversed") |

### 10.2 The worst-screen rule

Both men started from the poorest rendering surface and let identity survive there first.

- **Mac, 1984.** A one-bit screen. Chicago with no diagonals.
- **Slime, 1986.** A Famicom sprite. A teardrop with two dots and a curve.
- **Kurita's emoji, 1998–99.** A 12×12 grid, 144 pixels, 18 bytes per glyph, 176 glyphs in just over 3 KB.
- **iPod, 2001.** A small monochrome screen; Chicago appears on early models.

Apple's own history proves the corollary. Its glossy 2008 emoji, designed for a larger canvas, had to be rerendered in 2016 to display at a larger scale. The Slime has needed no redesign. A shape built for the narrowest pipe survives every wider pipe that follows; a shape built for a wide pipe dates the moment the pipe changes.

### 10.3 The empty face and the learned alphabet

Scott McCloud's *Understanding Comics* argues that minimal, iconic faces let readers project themselves into a character, and identifies a "masking effect" in Japanese comics: simple characters against realistically rendered objects. Toriyama is a textbook case. Two-dot Slimes and circle-eyed Goku move through a world that contains an accurately drawn Horten Ho 229 flying wing, a Reliant Scimitar, a Ferrari Dino hovercar.

Neil Cohn's research complicates the claim usefully. With Sean Ehly he catalogued 73 conventionalized graphic schemas (sweat drops, nose bubbles, anger marks) that form a learned "visual vocabulary" within a "Japanese Visual Language," and his EEG experiments show that scrambled comic panels trigger the same brain response as ungrammatical sentences. So two layers operate at two speeds:

- **The learned layer** is local and conventional: a sweat drop means nervousness.
- **The pre-linguistic layer** is a silhouette, a contour, two dots and a curve. It needs no instruction.

Both men put their originality in the second layer and kept the first conventional. Geneva is "a redesigned Helvetica." Goku's story is *Journey to the West*. And both used Japanese woodblock-print logic (flat color, bounding line) for their opening screen or page.

### 10.4 Cinema as a framing device

- **Jobs** bought Pixar, launched the Mac with a Ridley Scott commercial, and treated every keynote as a reveal.
- **Toriyama** plays stories out in his head as movie scenes, watched kung-fu films while inking, and rebuilt his drawing hand after studying the animator Toyo'o Ashida, saying his speed rose "by about a factor of 15." His most consequential pacing decision was giving characters flight to skip travel scenes the way an editor would.

Both learned from film that the audience should never wait.

### 10.5 Persona and process

| | Jobs | Toriyama |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Public role** | Keynote performer | Recluse; a robot avatar in print from 1980 |
| **Work style** | Team-based, directive | "Start-to-finish by myself" with at most one assistant |
| **Credit** | Wanted credit for taste | "The truth is, I didn't like being a manga artist very much" (2010) |
| **Relationship to the work** | Obsessive over details, including names and packaging | Practical about effort, obsessive about line and machinery |
| **Collaborators of note** | Susan Kare, Bill Atkinson, Andy Hertzfeld, Jony Ive, Paul Rand, Hartmut Esslinger | Kazuhiko Torishima, Yuji Horii, Koichi Sugiyama, Nachi Mikami, Toyotarou |
| **Home life** | Wife Laurene Powell, married March 18, 1991 | Wife Yoshimi Katō (Nachi Mikami), married May 2, 1982; two children |

### 10.6 The role of the wife

Mikami's role is small but concrete. She shared Toriyama's interest in Chinese settings, and by Toriyama's account she suggested the name **Kamehameha** for Goku's signature attack, as a joke, which he kept. A first, near-faithful draft of *Journey to the West* reportedly got a poor reaction from her and others, which pushed him toward a looser adaptation. Laurene Powell Jobs, for her part, built her own philanthropic and media enterprises. The comparison is not equivalent: one is a studio collaborator at the edge of the page, the other a partner in a separate career.

### 10.7 Japan and the West

| Direction | Jobs | Toriyama |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Imported** | Zen (Kōbun), Sony (Akio Morita's Walkman), Issey Miyake's turtlenecks, Kyoto gardens, Hasui and Goyō woodblock prints | Disney, Lucas, Ridley Scott, Jackie Chan, Bruce Lee, Frazetta, German and American tank kits |
| **Exported** | Hardware, interface, and emoji reach | Manga grammar, shōnen template |
| **What he refused** | Sony's "high-tech" styling ("It's easy to do that. But it's not great.") | The idea that he was an artist rather than a manga worker |
| **Misattributed to him** | *Ma* (negative space) guiding Apple design; no Jobs quotation uses the term | The poop emoji, and any statement about Nintendo or Zelda |

In March 1983 Jobs, Wozniak, and Rod Holt visited the Watanabe gallery in Ginza. Jobs began collecting Kawase Hasui prints and, by the Mac launch, had a 1920 Hashiguchi Goyō print as the screen's demo image. The medium that taught Japan to think in flat color and bounding line was the demo graphic for the first mass-market bitmap display.

### 10.8 Constraint as specification

| Constraint | Maker | Outcome |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 128 KB, one bit per pixel | Mac | Chicago, with no diagonals |
| A $6,995 printer shared by sixteen users | LaserWriter | Desktop publishing |
| No money for screentone | Toriyama | Solid blacks, the poster idiom |
| Fourteen pages a week | Toriyama | The flight rule, invented worlds, blank hair |
| 64 KB total for the game, music included | *Dragon Quest* | Single hero, a tanka-form password, a locked throne room |
| 12×12 pixels | Kurita | 18-byte glyphs |
| 250-character emails | i-mode | The reason emoji existed |

None of them negotiated the constraint away. Each treated it as the specification.

## 11. Where Their Work Physically Meets

The two lineages cross in at least five places, and one of them is a small smiling pile of excrement.

### 11.1 Typography and the 1984 Macintosh

The Mac's bitmap fonts were drawn by Susan Kare and Bill Atkinson. Jobs's documented typographic acts are the renaming ("world class cities"), the push for proportional type, and the PostScript deal, not the drawing. Chicago was the system font until Mac OS 8 and reappeared on early iPods. Toriyama's lettering, forced on him at a design firm, was the skill that got him noticed. Two men, two kinds of hand-lettering, one rule: letters are functional objects judged by how they move the eye.

### 11.2 Emoji

**The record, in order:**

1. **1980–84.** *Dr. Slump* runs. Its recurring character **Unchi-kun**, "Poop-Boy," is one of several coiled feces that look like soft-serve ice cream, with "a sense of justice" and feet for running. A cute, anthropomorphic poop becomes ordinary in Japanese pop culture.
2. **November 1, 1997.** J-Phone (later SoftBank) releases the SkyWalker DP-211SW with **90 monochrome 12×12 emoji, including a smiling pile of poo.** Designers unknown. This was the first mobile emoji set, a fact Emojipedia's Jeremy Burge formally corrected on March 8, 2019.
3. **1998–99.** Shigetaka Kurita, 25, designs 176 emoji for NTT DoCoMo's i-mode in about five weeks, using manga's symbolic marks (*manpu*) as his vocabulary. He wanted a poop glyph; DoCoMo said "no good."
4. **October 2008.** Gmail emoji launch. Google's team fought headquarters over the animated poop; a Japanese product manager, Takeshi Kishimoto, cited *Dr. Slump* as the reason it was popular.
5. **November 21, 2008.** iPhone OS 2.2 ships Apple's first emoji font, built on SoftBank's encoding because SoftBank was Apple's Japanese partner. That is why Apple's and Unicode's poop descends from the 1997 handset rather than from DoCoMo's set.
6. **October 2010.** Unicode 6.0 standardizes 722 emoji, including U+1F4A9.
7. **2011–2015.** iOS 5 opens the keyboard (2011); Apple moves to Unicode code points (2012); the keyboard is on by default (2015).
8. **2015–2016.** Oxford names a pictograph its Word of the Year; MoMA acquires Kurita's set.

### 11.3 What can and cannot be said

| Link | Status |
| :--- | :--- |
| *Dr. Slump*'s Unchi-kun normalized a cute poop in Japanese culture | Documented cultural priming |
| A Google product manager cited *Dr. Slump* to justify shipping the glyph | Documented, on the record, November 2014 |
| Toriyama designed the poop emoji | **Myth.** No Toriyama statement about emoji exists |
| Jobs championed emoji on the iPhone | **Unsupported.** Emoji shipped on his watch, but no Jobs statement or decision about them is recorded |
| The 1997 glyph's designer drew from Unchi-kun | No source identifies the designer or links the glyph to him |

The accurate claim is about idiom: Toriyama established a visual idiom (the smiling swirl) so thoroughly that a decade later an anonymous designer could draw it on a 12×12 grid and have it read at once. That is cultural authorship of a visual language, which is real and worth more than designing one glyph. Jobs's position is the mirror image. He designed no emoji, but the product he left carried the idiom to the planet.

### 11.4 Kurita's complaint

Kurita's later view of what emoji became is skeptical in a way that matters here: "Contemporary emoji aren't really emoji… the majority of them are simply pictures." A pictogram that fits 144 pixels is a character. A rendered illustration is a picture. By 2025 the set exceeded 3,700 characters. The compression instinct that made 176 glyphs work has little role in a catalogue that large.

### 11.5 The Macintosh in Toriyama's studio

In December 1996 Toriyama began coloring digitally on a Macintosh. It is the one documented place where his daily tools and Jobs's, newly returned and a year from reviving Apple, touch. Nothing is recorded of either man commenting on it.

### 11.6 Games

Jobs's NeXT machines helped build *Doom* and *Quake*; Toriyama's pen designed the monsters that first reached Japanese children on the Famicom. Different worlds, same year-to-year question: what is the fewest marks that still carry the identity?

## 12. The Monkey King Lens

*Journey to the West* is attributed to Wu Cheng'en (c. 1500 to c. 1582), though it was published anonymously in the 1590s and grew out of centuries of folk tales about the monk Xuanzang's real seventh-century journey to India. Toriyama's *Dragon Ball* borrows its cast, with changes:

| *Journey to the West* | *Dragon Ball* |
| :--- | :--- |
| Sun Wukong, stone-born, with a staff (*Ruyi Jingu Bang*) and a flying cloud | Son Goku, with a tail, the Power Pole (*Nyoibo*), and the Flying Nimbus (*Kinto'un*) |
| Tang Sanzang, the vulnerable monk leading the quest | Bulma, the brilliant, physically vulnerable girl with a radar |
| Zhu Bajie, the shapeshifting pig | Oolong, a shapeshifting pig who is a coward |
| Sha Wujing, the sand demon | Yamcha, a desert bandit |

The adaptation is Toriyama's own: in his 1995 interview he says he decided to base the story on *Journey to the West*, while his editor pushed for a kung-fu manga. The novel is a path of trials toward a goal; early *Dragon Ball* follows it before Toriyama replaces the pilgrimage with a ladder of tournaments.

### Reading the two lives through the myth

This is interpretation, offered as a lens rather than a claim of fact.

- **The stone.** Both men came from nowhere in particular. Stone has no parents.
- **The peaches.** Wukong's crime is taking the immortal peaches of Heaven. Jobs's is the Mac, an uninvited intrusion into a business that belonged to others. Toriyama's is *Dr. Slump*, a hit he did not plan and did not want to continue.
- **The Five Elements Mountain.** Buddha pins Wukong under it. The 1985 hinge pins each man under a different mountain: Jobs under exile, Toriyama under success.
- **Five hundred.** Wukong is trapped for five hundred years. Toriyama's apprenticeship was about five hundred rejected pages. The coincidence is accidental and still instructive: both numbers measure how long a rebel spends under pressure before being useful.
- **The headband.** Xuanzang controls Wukong with a golden band that tightens when he chants a spell. Jobs's headband was the board and, later, Markkula's three words; Toriyama's was the reader survey and Torishima's no. Neither man would have shipped what he shipped without it.
- **The scripture.** The pilgrimage's goal is not for the pilgrim; it is for others. Jobs's was the iPod and iPhone. Toriyama's was a generation of children who learned to read comics from a monkey boy.
- **Enlightenment versus a stronger opponent.** Sun Wukong ends in Buddhahood after learning restraint. Goku never does. Jobs, in his last years, spoke of focus and death; Toriyama, in his last years, delegated and kept drawing machines. If there is a Wukong in this pair, it is Jobs. If there is a Goku, it is Toriyama.

## 13. Twelve Lessons for Second Acts

Each rule is stated with the evidence that earns it.

1. **A forced fork is a gift if you keep your ruler.** Jobs lost his title and kept his standard; Toriyama gained a second job and kept his. The standard is portable; the title is not.
2. **Build the next thing in a medium the old thing never touched.** Pixar for Jobs; *Dragon Quest* for Toriyama. A side project in a different medium teaches what the main project has stopped teaching.
3. **Keep an outside judge, or install one.** NeXT lacked it and shipped a beautiful machine nobody could afford; *Dragon Ball* had it and shipped the biggest series of its decade.
4. **Treat price and deadline as design parameters.** The $1,299 iMac and the weekly fourteen pages are the same kind of constraint.
5. **Make the first screen the lesson.** Both the Mac's opening image and the Slime's first appearance carry the whole identity of the thing.
6. **Remove noise, never signal.** The fan that is not there; the hair that is not inked.
7. **Spend what simplification saves where the user is looking.** Box and fight scene.
8. **Keep the mascot stable while the cast changes.** The Slime, Chicago, the red heart.
9. **Own the idiom, not the artifact.** Dr. Slump's swirl outlived any one glyph; Apple's keyboard outlived any one emoji.
10. **Know when to hand off.** Toriyama gave the weekly page to Toyotarou; Jobs gave the company to Cook. Both did it while working.
11. **Let the work be the proof.** Kōbun's demand for proof and Toriyama's refusal of any title grander than a gag-manga artist agree.
12. **Return changed.** Jobs returned with fewer products. Toriyama returned with less drawing. In both cases less was the point.

## 16. Closing

The conventional stories say taste made Apple and laziness made *Dragon Ball*. The pairing here suggests something simpler and harder. In 1985, two thirty-year-olds with the same procedure (a ruler from next door, a judge with a number, and a ceiling low enough that only the identity-bearing marks survived) were each handed a fork. Jobs lost his title and kept his standard. Toriyama gained a job he did not want and kept his. Each spent a decade under his own mountain doing work that looked, from outside, like a detour. In both cases the detour built the next thing: NeXTSTEP and Pixar became the core of the Apple of 1997; *Dragon Quest* and the weekly grind became the Slime and the shōnen template.

The returns differed because the men did. Jobs came back to concentrate. Toriyama came back, when he did, to let go. One built an institution that would pass to a successor; the other left a set of shapes that younger hands could keep drawing. Both would have understood the same sentence from a Zen priest and a manga editor: no claim counts until the object exists.

And both objects are still in circulation. In October 2026 the Slime is a forty-year-old teardrop and the iPhone is a nineteen-year-old rectangle, and neither needs to be explained to a stranger. A flyer has no reader, only a passer-by. Every one of the things described here was drawn for the passer-by. The passer-by turned out to be everyone.
